## Metrics

To have a meaningful discussion about architectures, we need **metrics** to evaluate them, and to know how those metrics are affected by **Moore's Law**: devices per chip double every 18 to 24 months. It is slowing, but it has been a significant historical trend.

| Metric | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Performance** | The focus of this course |
| **Cost** | |
| **Power** | Important; some exposure in the labs |
| **Reliability** | |

Metrics are often more important in combination than on their own: performance/cost (MIPS/$) and performance/power (MIPS/W, or GOPS/W for a TPU). They are the basis for both design decisions and purchasing decisions.

## Latency vs Throughput

| Metric | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Latency** (execution time) | Time required to finish a fixed task |
| **Throughput** (bandwidth) | Number of tasks completed in a fixed time |

- They are very different: throughput can exploit parallelism, latency cannot (the bread-baking analogy).
- They are often contradictory.
- Choose the definition that matches your goals. Most often, that is throughput.

### Example: car vs bus

Move people from A to B, 10 miles away:

| | Capacity | Speed | Latency | Throughput |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Car** | 5 | 60 mph | 10 min | 15 people/hour |
| **Bus** | 60 | 20 mph | 30 min | 60 people/hour |

The car's latency is 10 miles / 60 mph = 10 min, and it makes 3 round trips per hour, carrying 5 people each time.

## Comparing Performance

Processor A is **X times faster** than B if:

$$
\text{Latency}(P,A) = \frac{\text{Latency}(P,B)}{X}
$$

$$
\text{Throughput}(P,A) = \text{Throughput}(P,B) \times X
$$

Processor A is **X% faster** than B if:

$$
\text{Latency}(P,A) = \frac{\text{Latency}(P,B)}{1 + \frac{X}{100}}
$$

$$
\text{Throughput}(P,A) = \text{Throughput}(P,B) \times \left(1 + \frac{X}{100}\right)
$$

For the car and bus:

- **Latency:** the car is 3 times (200%) faster than the bus.
- **Throughput:** the bus is 4 times (300%) faster than the car.

## Benchmarks: What Is P?

$P$ is the **program**. "Latency(A)" makes no sense on its own: a processor executes _some_ program. But which one?

| Program | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Actual target workload** | Accurate | Not portable, not repeatable, overly specific<br />Hard to pinpoint problems |
| **Representative benchmark programs** | Portable and repeatable, pretty accurate | Hard to pinpoint problems<br />May not be exactly what you run |
| **Small kernel benchmarks** (micro-benchmarks) | Portable and repeatable, easy to run<br />Easy to pinpoint problems | Not representative of the complex behaviours in real programs |

## Adding and Averaging

You can add latencies, but not throughputs:

$$
\text{Latency}(P_1 + P_2, A) = \text{Latency}(P_1, A) + \text{Latency}(P_2, A)
$$

$$
\text{Throughput}(P_1 + P_2, A) \mathrel{\char"2260} \text{Throughput}(P_1, A) + \text{Throughput}(P_2, A)
$$

**Example:** drive 1 km at 30 kph, then 1 km at 90 kph. The average speed is **not** 60 kph. The two legs take 0.033 h and 0.011 h, so:

$$
\text{average} = \frac{1\text{ km} + 1\text{ km}}{0.033\text{ h} + 0.011\text{ h}} = 45\text{ kph}
$$

As a formula:

$$
\text{Throughput}(P_1 + P_2, A) = \frac{2}{\frac{1}{\text{Throughput}(P_1, A)} + \frac{1}{\text{Throughput}(P_2, A)}}
$$

The same goes for means (averages):

| Mean | Formula | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Arithmetic** | $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{P=1}^{N} \text{Latency}(P)$ | Units proportional to time, e.g. latency |
| **Harmonic** | $N \,/\, \sum_{P=1}^{N} \frac{1}{\text{Throughput}(P)}$ | Units inversely proportional to time, e.g. throughput |
| **Geometric** | $\sqrt[N]{\prod_{P=1}^{N} \text{Speedup}(P)}$ | Unitless quantities, e.g. speedups |

:::caution
Use the right average for the metric.
:::

## Iron Law of Performance

Performance has multiple aspects; the iron law isolates them:

$$
\text{Latency}(P, A) = \frac{\text{seconds}}{\text{program}} = \frac{\text{instructions}}{\text{program}} \times \frac{\text{cycles}}{\text{instruction}} \times \frac{\text{seconds}}{\text{cycle}}
$$

| Term | Is the | A function of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Instructions / program** | Dynamic instruction count | Program, compiler, ISA |
| **Cycles / instruction** | CPI | Program, compiler, ISA, **microarchitecture** |
| **Seconds / cycle** | Clock period | Microarchitecture, technology parameters |

For low latency (better performance), minimize all three. This is hard: they often pull against each other.

:::tip
To improve performance, optimize each component. This course focuses mostly on **CPI**.
:::

## Cycles per Instruction (CPI)

- CPI is the cycles per instruction for the **average instruction**.
- **IPC** = 1 / CPI.
- Different instructions have different cycle costs, e.g. an integer add typically takes 1 cycle, an FP divide takes more than 10.
- So CPI assumes you know something about the instruction frequencies: $\text{CPI} = \sum_i \text{frequency}_i \times \text{cycles}_i$.

:::caution
This kind of calculation ignores dependences completely: use it for back-of-the-envelope arguments only.
:::

### Example 1

A program executes equal numbers of integer, FP and memory operations. Integer takes 1 cycle, memory 2, FP 3.

$$
\text{CPI} = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 3 = 2
$$

### Example 2: Branch Prediction or a Bigger Cache?

| Instruction | Frequency | Cycles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Integer ALU | 50% | 1 |
| Load | 20% | 5 |
| Store | 10% | 1 |
| Branch | 20% | 2 |

Which change improves performance more?

- **A.** [Branch prediction](/ece552/branch-prediction/#branch-prediction), to reduce the branch cost to 1 cycle.
- **B.** A bigger data cache, to reduce the load cost to 3 cycles.

| | CPI |
| --- | --- |
| **Base** | $0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 5 + 0.1 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 2$ |
| **A** | $0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 5 + 0.1 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 1 = 1.8$ |
| **B** | $0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 3 + 0.1 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 1.6$ |

**B** wins.

### Example 3: CPI vs Clock

| Operation | Frequency | Cycles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ALU | 45% | 1 |
| Load | 20% | 1 |
| Store | 15% | 2 |
| Branch | 20% | 2 |

**A.** You can reduce stores to 1 cycle, but it slows the clock down by 20% (frequency 100% to 80%, so the period goes from $T$ to $T / 0.8$).

- Old CPI $= 0.45 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.15 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 1.35$
- New CPI $= 0.45 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.15 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 1.2$

$$
\text{Speedup} = \frac{\text{old time}}{\text{new time}} = \frac{IC \cdot 1.35 \cdot T}{IC \cdot 1.2 \cdot \frac{1}{0.8} T} = \frac{0.8 \cdot 1.35}{1.2} = 0.9
$$

A **slowdown**: the instruction count $IC$ cancels, and the CPI gain doesn't pay for the slower clock.

**B.** Now suppose ALU ops were originally 2 cycles and stores 1 cycle, and you could reduce ALU ops to 1 cycle, again slowing the clock by 20%.

- Old CPI $= 0.45 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.15 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 1.65$
- New CPI $= 0.45 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.15 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 1.2$

$$
\text{Speedup} = \frac{IC \cdot 1.65 \cdot T}{IC \cdot 1.2 \cdot \frac{1}{0.8} T} = 1.1
$$

A **speedup**.

:::tip
This is an example of **Amdahl's law**: don't speed up a small fraction to the detriment of the rest.
:::

## Measuring CPI

- **Execution time:** the Unix `time` command gives wall clock, CPU and system time.
- **CPI** = CPU time / (clock period $\times$ dynamic instruction count).
- We want **CPI breakdowns** ($\text{CPI}_\text{CPU}$, $\text{CPI}_\text{MEM}$, etc.) to see what to fix.

| Way to get a CPI breakdown | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Hardware event counters** | Calculate CPI from the counter frequencies and event costs |
| **Cycle-level microarchitecture simulation** (e.g. SimpleScalar, used in labs 0, 1, 3 and 4) | Measures the breakdown "exactly", provided it models the microarchitecture faithfully and runs realistic workloads. The method of choice for many microarchitects (and you) |

## Improving CPI

This course is more about improving CPI than frequency.

- **Historically**, the clock accounted for 70%+ of performance improvement, achieved with deeper pipelines.
- **This has changed:** deep pipelining is not power efficient, and physical speed limits are approaching. 1 GHz in 1999, 2 GHz in 2001, 3 GHz in 2002, 5 GHz in 2008; an Intel Core Ultra 9 (Arrow Lake, 2024) runs at 3.2 to 5.7 GHz.
- **Techniques in this course:** [caching](/ece552/caches/), [speculation](/ece552/speculation/), multiple issue, [out-of-order issue](/ece552/dynamic-scheduling/#motivation-dynamic-scheduling), multiprocessing, and more.

Moore's Law helps because reducing CPI requires transistors: parallelism by definition needs "more transistors", but the best example is caches.

## Amdahl's Law

The performance rule of thumb (and its tyranny):

- $f$: the fraction that can run in parallel (be sped up)
- $1 - f$: the fraction that must run serially

$$
\text{Speedup} = \frac{1}{(1 - f) + \frac{f}{n}}
\qquad
\lim_{n \to \infty} \text{Speedup} = \frac{1}{1 - f}
$$

<AmdahlCurve client:visible {...amdahlCores.props} />

- In this ideal model, scaling is pretty good for a modest number of cores.
- A large number of cores requires a lot of parallelism.

:::tip
Amdahl's law is not just about parallelism: $f$ is the fraction that you can speed up, by any means. Your performance will always be limited by the $1 - f$ part.
:::
